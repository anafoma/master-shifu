# The Muscle Ladder (Nippard, Dec 2024): structure and key defaults

A paraphrased map of the book's framework and its main numeric defaults, written for coaching decisions. It is not a substitute for the book: no text, tables or programmes are reproduced. Get the book for the full reasoning, exercise photos and the 20 programmes: [publisher](https://penguinrandomhouse.ca/books/717784/the-muscle-ladder-by-jeff-nippard/9781628604863).

## The ladder

Two rails hold the ladder up. Ten rungs are climbed in order: lower rungs matter more and must be in place before the higher ones are worth optimising.

## Rails

**Sustainability:** three tests: does the user enjoy it, is it safe, and is the pace right (not so slow they lose interest, not so fast they burn out)? If the gym isn't someone's happy place, cut the programme down to what they dislike least rather than forcing more (minimalist options: shorter sessions or fewer days). Watch for overreaching signs: persistent joint aches, strength loss, exhaustion, extreme lasting soreness, lost motivation.

**Mindset:** set realistic expectations for muscle gain (it slows with training age), stop comparing yourself to others, and build a goal hierarchy (big outcome goal → intermediate goals → daily behaviours such as sleeping 7+ hours). Track progress three ways: strength performance, standardised progress photos (same poses, light, time), and bodyweight trend.

## Rungs, condensed to Shifu defaults

1. **Technique:** controlled, full range, consistent form; the book has photo guides for 80+ exercises.
2. **Exercise selection:** pick exercises by stretch, feel and progression (see [[exercise-tiers]]). Vary exercises across the week for the same muscle (e.g. back squat one day, hack or front squat the next; flat barbell bench then incline dumbbell), because mixed exercises grew the quads more evenly than squats alone in a study he cites.
3. **Effort:** most hypertrophy sets at RPE 7–10 (0–3 RIR), scaled by exercise type:
   - **Primary heavy compounds** (squat, bench, deadlift, heavy presses): RPE 6–8 most of the time, rising across sets (e.g. 6 → 7 → 8). Push to 9–10 only rarely (every few months) to calibrate.
   - **Secondary compounds** (pulldowns, rows, pull-ups, lunges, hip thrusts): RPE 8–10; the last set can go to failure.
   - **Isolation** (curls, pressdowns, lateral raises): RPE 9–10 most of the time.
   - Distinguish **technical failure** (can't do another rep with the same form) from absolute failure; aim for technical. If reps aren't slowing down, the set isn't close to failure.
4. **Progressive overload:** nine strategies: (1) add load (linear), (2) add reps at the same load, (3) double progression, (4) add sets, (5) better technique, (6) faster bar speed at the same load, (7) better mind-muscle connection, (8) shorter rest, (9) extend sets past failure. Default: linear for beginners, then double progression. Use ~5 lb (2.5 kg) jumps; micro-plates rarely needed. Mind-muscle focus suits isolation and higher-rep work; for heavy low-rep compounds, use an external focus.
5. **Volume (sets per muscle per week):** beginners ~10 (even 1–5 gives some gains); intermediate 10–20; advanced 10–20, with brief experiments above 20 for stubborn muscles and rarely above 30. Maintenance: 4–8. Back, glutes, shoulders and quads tolerate the most; chest slightly less; **hamstrings respond best to lower volume and most people struggle to recover beyond ~12–15 sets/week**; biceps/triceps counted as isolation sets only, and beginners need little direct arm work while advanced lifters need more.
6. **Splits and frequency:** his single favourite split for size and strength is **upper/lower** (balanced development, each muscle 2×/week). Full body 2–3×/week suits beginners; very high-frequency full body isn't recommended. Spread volume: the more sets a muscle needs, the less sense it makes to cram them into one session.
7. **Load and rep ranges:** anything from about 3 to 30 reps grows muscle when sets are hard; below ~20% 1RM is too light and 1–2-rep sets are worse for growth. Practical split of weekly sets: **60–80% moderate (6–15 reps), 10–20% low (1–5), 10–20% high (15–30)**.
8. **Rest:** isolation 1–2 min; light compounds 2–3 min; **heavy compounds 3–5 min**. Most recovery happens in the first minute, but too-short rest reduces volume done.
9. **Advanced techniques** (only once rungs 1–8 are solid): partial reps (lengthened), drop sets, myo-reps, loaded stretching. Each has cautions; use them on safe exercises (machines, cables, isolation), sparingly.
10. **Periodisation:** build volume and/or intensity over a 4–8-week mesocycle, then deload for a week. Options: linear, reverse linear, weekly undulating. Deloads:
    - **Full break** only when needed (strength holds for 2–3 weeks of detraining).
    - **Calculated deload:** cut volume 30–50% (usually 1–2 fewer sets per exercise), drop effort by about 1–3 RPE, or reduce %1RM by 5–10%.
    - **Instinctive deload:** a planned "light week" with flexibility: fewer sets, further from failure, swap exercises that felt like a grind, switch barbell to dumbbells or machines.
    - He typically plans a deload roughly every 8 weeks, or takes one when weights feel brutally heavy, sleep slips and motivation drops. Beginners not yet training hard don't need deloads.

## Nutrition chapter (summary)

Consistent with [[nutrition]]: estimate maintenance (quick formula, Mifflin-St Jeor, or guess-and-check against the weight trend), then set a surplus or deficit. Supplements: protein powder (convenience), creatine and caffeine are worthwhile; fish oil has solid health evidence but no consistent muscle effect; multivitamins do little for healthy people unless covering a deficiency (e.g. some vegetarian or vegan diets); L-citrulline is one of the few newer performance supplements whose claims hold up reasonably.
